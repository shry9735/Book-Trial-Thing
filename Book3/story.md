## Before You Launch

This isn't a book you read from front to back. **You** are on the team. Every few pages, you'll make a choice, and the choice sends you to a different page.

Some choices pay off right away. Others don't seem to matter at all... until hours later, thirty kilometers up, or in the middle of a muddy field with the sun going down. That's how it works in real engineering. The decisions you make on the workbench are the ones that fly.

There are **15 different endings**. Some are triumphs. Some are disasters. Nobody gets hurt in any of them, but things can go very wrong, and when they do, it's never just bad luck. Every ending happens *because* of something you chose.

When you reach an ending, erase your Mission Log and start again. Try to find all fifteen.

## Meet Skylark

Skylark is a weather-balloon payload: a small foam box full of electronics that rides a balloon to the edge of space and parachutes back down.

| Part | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Arduino** | Skylark's brain. A tiny computer that runs Bitsy's code and controls everything else |
| **GPS module** | Listens to satellites to work out exactly where Skylark is, and how high |
| **Radio + antenna** | Sends a packet of data down to the chase van every ten seconds |
| **Pressure & temperature sensor** | Measures the air outside. Less pressure means higher up |
| **Camera** | Takes the pictures, saved to its own memory card |
| **Memory card logger** | Saves every reading, so nothing is lost even if the radio misses it |
| **Beeper** | Very loud. Turns on near the ground, so you can hear Skylark in a field |
| **4 AA batteries** | Power for everything |
| **Foam box** | Keeps out the cold, and keeps everything together |
| **Parachute** | Brings Skylark down gently after the balloon bursts |
| **Weather balloon** | Thin latex, filled with helium. Starts the size of a car and stretches to the size of a house before it bursts |

> 🖼️ **Illustration · page_009** — *Mr. Torres stacks chairs at the edge of the maker space, while in the foreground a phone glows with a text-message conversation of simple blank speech bubbles. Spark…*

8:40. Mr. Torres is starting to stack chairs, which is his way of saying *twenty minutes*.

You look at Bitsy. Bitsy looks at Spark.

Bitsy's house is five minutes away. Her mom said you could all stay until midnight. You could set Skylark on her kitchen table, run it for the full three hours, and see what happens. But it'd be after midnight before you're home, and launch is at nine.

Or you could switch it on for five minutes, see it boot, see a packet, and go to sleep.

> **ENGINEERING NOTE — Soak Testing**
> A soak test means running a system for as long as the real job, or longer, before you trust it. Some problems only show up over time: memory filling up, batteries draining, parts warming up, a card running out of space. A five-minute test proves the system can start. A soak test proves it can keep going. Many real spacecraft failures were problems that only appeared hours into the mission.

→ **[Page 15](#page-15)** — Run the full three-hour soak test at Bitsy's house.

## Page 11

### When Should It Beep?

> 🖼️ **Illustration · page_011** — *A small round black piezo beeper on a tiny circuit board in the foreground; behind it Bitsy and Spark argue good-naturedly, D.U.D.E.A.D. between them with a raised hand…*

The beeper is the last line of defense. When Skylark lands in a field, the radio will tell you roughly where it is, but "roughly" might mean a cornfield the size of four football fields. The beeper is loud and high-pitched, and you can hear it from about fifty meters away.

It can't beep the whole flight, though. It would drain the battery and drive everyone crazy. Bitsy's plan is to turn it on when Skylark falls back below 2 kilometers.

"So the question is, which altitude?" she says. "The pressure sensor gives us one. The GPS gives us another. I want it to check *both*. If either one says we're below 2 kilometers on the way down, turn on the beeper."

Spark groans. "Pressure updates faster. One sensor, one line. Done. Why make it complicated?"

"It's one `or`."

"It's one more thing to go wrong."

D.U.D.E.A.D. raises an eyebrow, or the part of his visor where an eyebrow would be. "Ask yourself what happens if that one sensor is wrong," he says.

> **ENGINEERING NOTE — Redundancy**
> Redundancy means having more than one way to do something important, so a single failure can't stop it. Airplanes have more than one engine and more than one altimeter. The trick is that the backups have to be truly *different*. Two GPS readings fail the same way. A pressure altitude and a GPS altitude work by completely different methods, so it's very unlikely both will be wrong at the same moment.

→ **[Page 21](#page-21)** — Pressure altitude only. It updates faster.

→ **[Page 12](#page-12)** — Either one. Whichever says 'below 2 km' first.

> **ENGINEERING NOTE — Free Lift**
> A balloon rises when the helium inside it is lighter than the air it pushes out of the way. The difference is called lift. Lift that exactly equals the payload's weight just hovers. *Extra* lift, called free lift, is what makes it climb, and Newton's second law says more extra force means more acceleration. Too little free lift and it climbs slowly and might never burst. Too much and it climbs fast but bursts low.

> **ENGINEERING NOTE — Latex and Skin Oil**

> **ENGINEERING NOTE — Inrush Current**
> When electronics first switch on, they briefly draw much more current than when they're running, as everything charges up and starts at once. That surge is called inrush current. A strong battery handles it easily. A weak or frozen battery can't, and its voltage dips so low that the device shuts down mid-startup, then tries again, and again: a boot loop.

> **ENGINEERING NOTE — Receiver Overload**
> A receiver is built to pick up very faint signals. When the transmitter is close, the signal is so strong that it floods in from every direction, even through the "deaf" sides of a directional antenna. It's like trying to tell which direction a shout is coming from while someone shouts right into your ear. Radio hunters fix it by making the signal weaker on purpose: turning down the gain, or using their own body as a shield.
