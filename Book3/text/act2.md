=== A2-OPEN ===

Saturday, 7:30 am. The county fairgrounds are empty except for you, a few crows, and a very large tank of helium chained to the back of Mr. Torres's van.

There's frost on the grass. Your breath makes clouds. Bitsy is wearing two hats.

You spread a big blue tarp in the middle of the field so the balloon won't touch anything sharp. Spark sets up a folding table with a kitchen scale, a roll of tape and the box. D.U.D.E.A.D. stands at the edge of the tarp with his hands on his hips, like a coach.

Mr. Torres holds up his phone so everyone can see the forecast. "Calm now," he says. "Breezy by ten. Gusty by ten-thirty. Once the gusts start, a balloon this size is a giant sail. We don't launch in that."

So you have a window: right now until 10:30. Every fifteen minutes you spend is one box gone.

Find the **Launch** clock on your Mission Log. It has six boxes. When a page tells you to cross one out, do it. If all six get crossed out, the window closes.

=== A2-PREDICT ===

Bitsy unfolds a sheet of paper on the table and flattens it with her mittens. It's a map, printed last night, with a curvy line drawn across it.

"This is where Skylark should go," she says. "I ran it through a flight predictor. You tell it when you launch, how fast you climb and when you burst, and it uses the wind forecast to work out the path."

The line runs northeast across the county and ends in a small oval in farmland, about forty kilometers away. The oval is the *landing ellipse*, the area where Skylark will probably come down.

"Predicted from Friday night's forecast," she adds.

Spark is looking at his phone. "The weather service put out a new forecast at six this morning."

"Winds don't change that much overnight," Bitsy says. But she doesn't sound totally sure.

Rerunning the prediction takes five minutes and Bitsy's phone. The old map is already printed.

> **ENGINEERING NOTE — Prediction**
> A prediction is only as good as the information you feed it. Winds high in the sky are measured by weather balloons launched twice a day all over the world. Forecasters use those measurements to predict the winds for the next few days, and the predictions get updated as new data comes in. Wind at 20 km can blow at over 100 km/h, so even a small change in the forecast can move a landing by many kilometers.

=== A2-OLDPREDICT ===

"Use last night's," you say. "We already have it printed."

Bitsy folds the map and slides it onto the van's dashboard, where everyone can see it. Northeast. Forty kilometers. Farmland, a few small roads, a creek.

Spark looks at his phone one more time, then puts it in his pocket.

"Northeast it is," he says.

Somewhere above you, at ten and twenty and thirty kilometers up, the wind is blowing exactly the way the six o'clock forecast said it would. It doesn't know about the map on the dashboard.

=== A2-REPREDICT ===

"Rerun it," you say.

Bitsy types the morning's numbers into the predictor on her phone. Same launch time, same climb rate, same burst altitude. Only the wind forecast is new.

The new line appears on top of the old one. It starts at the same place, the fairgrounds, and heads northeast the same way... and then it bends. Fifteen kilometers farther east.

Bitsy zooms in on the new landing ellipse. Then she zooms in some more. Then she stops.

Half of the oval is farmland. The other half is blue.

"Clearwater Reservoir," Mr. Torres reads over her shoulder. "That's a big lake."

"Half the ellipse," Bitsy says. "Half."

D.U.D.E.A.D. looks at the map, then at the sky, then back at the map. "Good thing you looked," he says. "Now what?"

=== A2-LAKE ===

There are three things you can change about a balloon flight. Bitsy runs them through the predictor one at a time while everyone crowds around her phone.

**More helium.** A fuller balloon climbs faster, so it spends less time drifting on the wind. It also pops lower, because it starts out bigger and reaches its stretching limit sooner. The new line lands short of the reservoir, at the edge of a state forest. Burst at about 27 kilometers instead of 31. The sky should still be black up there.

**Wait.** The forecast says the winds up high will swing north over the next hour. Launch at 10:15 instead of 9:15 and the landing moves into open farmland, but you'd be launching fifteen minutes before the gusts arrive.

**Do nothing.** Half of the ellipse is dry land.

> **ENGINEERING NOTE — Ascent Rate and Drift**
> A balloon drifts with the wind the whole time it's in the air. The longer it's up, the farther it drifts. If it climbs faster, it gets up and down sooner and drifts less, but a faster climb usually means more helium, and more helium means it reaches its bursting size sooner. Changing one number always changes others. Engineers call these trade-offs, and they're in every design.

=== A2-MOREGAS ===

"More helium," you say.

Bitsy updates the fill target on her clipboard: a little more lift than planned. She runs the prediction one more time to be sure. The new landing ellipse sits completely on land, short of the reservoir by a comfortable four kilometers.

Then she zooms out a little, and everyone sees what's on the other side of the ellipse: a dark green block on the map labeled HOLLIS STATE FOREST. The edge of the ellipse just touches it.

Nobody says the word *trees* out loud.

"Burst at twenty-seven kilometers," Bitsy says brightly instead. "Still black sky. Still the curve."

Spark nods. "Land's better than water."

D.U.D.E.A.D. studies the green block on the map for a long moment, then turns away and starts unpacking the balloon.

=== A2-WAIT ===

"We wait," you say.

So you wait. You sit on the edge of the tarp. Bitsy reruns the prediction every fifteen minutes, and every time the landing ellipse creeps a little farther north, away from the water. Spark eats two granola bars. Mr. Torres reads a newspaper with his back to the wind.

At 9:50, the ellipse is completely clear of the reservoir: open farmland, a few roads, no water anywhere near it.

"Got it," Bitsy says.

Then the newspaper in Mr. Torres's hands flaps hard, once, and he folds it up. The flag on the fairgrounds pole, which has been hanging limp all morning, lifts and stays lifted.

"Breeze is here," he says. "Let's move."

=== A2-HOPE ===

"Launch as planned," you say. "Half the ellipse is dry land. Most balloons land on land."

"Half," Bitsy says.

"Half is half," Spark says. "It's a coin flip, and we've got a good coin."

Bitsy doesn't answer. She saves the new prediction to her phone, the one with the blue oval, and puts the phone in her pocket without showing it to anyone again.

D.U.D.E.A.D. watches her do it. He doesn't say anything either.

Spark claps his hands. "Okay! Balloon!"

=== A2-SPARE ===

Bitsy unzips her backpack and pulls out a fat, silver USB power bank, the kind you charge your phone with.

"I brought this for backup," she says. "Fully charged. If the main batteries die, it can keep the Arduino running. We tape it in, plug it in, done. More power, more safety."

Spark eyes it. "How heavy is that?"

Here's the thing about weather balloons: there are rules. In the United States, a payload box like Skylark can fly without special permission from the government as long as it's light, under about four pounds (1.8 kg). Heavier than that, and you'd need paperwork you don't have.

"It's not heavy," Bitsy says. "It's like a phone."

The kitchen scale is sitting right there on the folding table.

> **ENGINEERING NOTE — Weight Limits**
> Rules for things that fly exist so that if something falls on someone, it can't hurt them. A light foam box under a parachute lands about as hard as a dropped pillow. Engineers working with a limit keep a weight budget: a list of every part and what it weighs, added up before anything gets built. Then every new idea has to fit inside the budget, no matter how good the idea is.

=== A2-WEIGH-FIRST ===

"Weigh it first," you say.

Spark sets Skylark gently on the kitchen scale with the lid taped on. The numbers flicker, then settle.

**1.74 kg.**

Then he sets the power bank on the scale by itself. **0.12 kg.**

Bitsy does the math out loud. "One seventy-four plus point one two is... one eighty-six." She looks at the limit written on her clipboard. 1.80. "Oh."

"Six hundredths over," Spark says. "That's one granola bar too heavy."

Bitsy puts the power bank back in her backpack. "Okay," she says. "Backup stays on the ground. Which is where we are. So it's backing *us* up."

=== A2-HEAVY ===

"Tape it in," you say.

Bitsy finds a spot next to the Arduino, plugs in the cable and wraps the bank in two loops of tape so it can't rattle. Then Spark seals the lid for real: four strips of tape on each edge, all the way around.

Then he sets the finished box on the scale, because that's the last step on the checklist.

**1.86 kg.**

The limit is 1.80.

Nobody says anything for a moment. Then Spark starts peeling tape off the lid, slowly, strip by strip, because the tape is the strong kind and it doesn't want to come off. It takes ten minutes to get back inside.

Sixty grams have to come out. Everyone stares into the box at the same time.

=== A2-STRIP-BANK ===

"The power bank," you say. "It's the newest thing in there. Everything else was planned."

Bitsy unplugs it and pulls it out. The scale reads **1.74 kg**, safely under.

"Fine," she says. "It *was* a good idea."

"It was a good idea," Spark agrees, taping the lid back down. "It just didn't fit."

D.U.D.E.A.D. nods. "Every part on the list had to earn its spot. That one showed up this morning without a ticket."

The power bank goes back in Bitsy's backpack, where it spends the rest of the day charging everybody's phones.

=== A2-STRIP-BEEP ===

"The beeper," you say. "We've got the radio. We don't need it to beep. And the hand warmer, if it's in there. That's the least important stuff."

Spark hesitates, then unclips the beeper board and its little battery. If there's a hand warmer in the box, it comes out too, still warm. He sets them on the table.

The scale settles just under the limit.

"Under," Bitsy says. "Barely."

Spark tapes the lid back on, slower this time. He keeps glancing at the beeper sitting on the folding table, silent.

"We've got the radio," he says, mostly to himself.

=== A2-STRIP-CAM ===

"The camera," you say. "It's the heaviest single thing. And we'll still have all the data."

Bitsy's face does something complicated. Then she nods and reaches in, unscrews the little camera board and its battery, and lifts them out.

**1.66 kg.** Easily under.

The lens hole in the side of the box is just a hole now. Bitsy covers it with a piece of tape.

"Data's the science part," Spark says, trying to help. "Graphs are science."

"Graphs are science," Bitsy agrees. She's quiet for a long time after that.

=== A2-GLOVES ===

Mr. Torres lifts the balloon out of its bag.

It doesn't look like a balloon yet. It looks like a deflated, pale tan beach ball the size of a bedsheet, folded up and dusted with powder. It's made of latex, the same stretchy material as party balloons, just thinner and much, much bigger.

There's a note on the bag in red letters: **WEAR GLOVES. SKIN OILS WEAKEN LATEX.**

The box of blue nitrile gloves is in the van. The van is parked at the far end of the lot, about five minutes away at a run, because nobody wanted it near the launch site.

"We'll be careful," Bitsy says. "Just hold it by the edges."

Mr. Torres is already holding the balloon with his bare hands. He looks down at them, then at you.

> **ENGINEERING NOTE — Latex and Skin Oil**
> Your skin is always covered in a thin layer of natural oil. Latex is made of long, stretchy molecules, and oils soak into it and weaken those molecules. A party balloon only stretches a little and doesn't notice. A weather balloon stretches to about six times its starting width before it bursts, and a weakened spot, like a fingerprint, can make it pop early. It's the same reason latex gloves come with "keep away from oil" warnings.

=== A2-GLOVED ===

"I'll get them," you say, and run.

The van is farther than it looked. You find the glove box under a seat, sprint back across the frozen grass with your lungs burning, and hand out pairs. Blue fingers everywhere. Mr. Torres has to try three sizes.

Ten minutes, there and back.

Spark wiggles his blue fingers at D.U.D.E.A.D. "You want some?"

"I don't have skin, kid," D.U.D.E.A.D. says. "I have *finish*."

Mr. Torres lays the balloon out on the tarp and hooks it up to the helium hose.

=== A2-BAREHAND ===

"Edges only," you say. "Carefully."

Mr. Torres nods and lays the balloon out on the tarp. You all help unfold it, pinching it at the very edges with your fingertips, the way you'd hold a photograph you don't want to smudge.

It's cold, and the latex is stiff and powdery. Bitsy's thumb slips once and presses flat against the middle of it for a second. She lifts it away quickly.

You can't see anything there. There's nothing to see. Just pale tan latex and a little powder.

Mr. Torres hooks the balloon up to the helium hose.

=== A2-FILL ===

Mr. Torres opens the valve on the tank. The hose hisses, and the balloon starts to rise off the tarp like something waking up.

Bitsy reads from her clipboard. "We fill it until it can lift Skylark's weight plus 1.2 kilograms extra. That extra is called *free lift*. It decides how fast it climbs."

She holds up a plastic jug of water with a hook on top. The weight is written on it in marker: the payload's weight plus free lift. "We hang this on the neck. When the balloon lifts the jug off the ground, it's exactly right."

The balloon gets bigger. And bigger. It's taller than Spark now, swaying gently.

"That looks plenty big," Mr. Torres says. "I've filled a lot of balloons for the county fair."

Spark is grinning. Bitsy, of all people, is grinning too. "Bigger balloon, higher flight, right?" she says. "Keep going!"

> **ENGINEERING NOTE — Free Lift**
> A balloon rises when the helium inside it is lighter than the air it pushes out of the way. The difference is called lift. Lift that exactly equals the payload's weight just hovers. *Extra* lift, called free lift, is what makes it climb, and Newton's second law says more extra force means more acceleration. Too little free lift and it climbs slowly and might never burst. Too much and it climbs fast but bursts low.

=== A2-FILL-RIGHT ===

"Jug," you say.

Spark hooks the water jug onto the balloon's neck. Mr. Torres keeps the helium flowing, slowly now. The jug sits on the tarp, heavy and still.

Then it twitches. Then it lifts, a centimeter, and hangs there, swaying, with its bottom just off the tarp.

"Close it!" Bitsy says, and Mr. Torres shuts the valve.

The jug hangs perfectly in the air. Not climbing. Not sinking. Exactly the right amount of helium to lift Skylark plus 1.2 kilograms.

"Five meters per second," Bitsy says. "That's what this makes. Right on plan."

D.U.D.E.A.D. gives a slow nod. "Measured," he says. "Not guessed. Nice."

=== A2-FILL-LOW ===

"That's big enough," you say. "Mr. Torres knows balloons."

Mr. Torres closes the valve. The balloon sways above the tarp, round and pale and tugging at its tether.

"Looks about right," he says.

It does look about right. It's huge, bigger than any balloon any of you have ever seen. The jug of water sits on the folding table, its hook still empty.

Spark pokes the side of the balloon gently with one gloved finger, and it bounces. "That's going *up*," he says.

It is. Not as fast as the plan said, but it is.

=== A2-FILL-HIGH ===

"Keep going!" you say.

Mr. Torres raises an eyebrow but keeps the valve open. The balloon keeps growing until it's taller than the van. It's pulling so hard on its tether that Spark has to hold the line with both hands, his sneakers sliding on the tarp.

"It wants to *leave*," he laughs.

Bitsy hangs the water jug on the neck for fun. It rips off the tarp and swings straight up. That's way past the mark.

"How fast does this one climb?" Spark asks.

Bitsy checks her clipboard and does some quick math. Her grin fades a little. "Really fast. And it'll get to bursting size a lot sooner."

Mr. Torres finally closes the valve. The balloon is enormous, and it isn't going to get any smaller.

=== A2-CHUTE ===

Between the balloon and Skylark hangs a parachute, a bright red circle of ripstop nylon about the size of an umbrella, with strings that meet at a little ring.

The order on the line goes: balloon at the top, then the parachute, then Skylark at the bottom. When the balloon bursts, Skylark starts to fall, and the air rushing past fills the chute and slows it down.

Bitsy has done something to it. She folded the chute neatly into a long, thin sleeve of fabric, like an umbrella in its cover, and closed it with a single wrap of tape.

"So it doesn't flap around during launch and tangle on the line," she says. "When Skylark falls, the air will pull it out of the sleeve."

Spark frowns at it. "Real balloon people leave the chute open. It flaps the whole way up. That's normal."

"Flapping is how things get tangled," Bitsy says.

> **ENGINEERING NOTE — Drag**
> Drag is the push air gives to anything moving through it. A big, open parachute catches a lot of air, so it gets a lot of drag and falls slowly. A folded one catches almost none. Skylark needs to come down at about 5 meters per second, about as fast as a dropped beach ball. Anything that stops the chute from opening fully, like a twist, a tangle or a sleeve that doesn't let go, means much less drag and a much faster fall.

=== A2-CHUTE-OPEN ===

"Open," you say. "That's how everyone else does it."

Bitsy peels the tape off her sleeve and shakes the chute out. It flops open on the tarp, a red circle. Spark ties it into the line with a knot Mr. Torres checks twice.

As the balloon sways above you, the chute catches the light breeze and flaps lazily, half open, like a flag that can't make up its mind.

"See?" Bitsy says. "Flapping."

"Flapping's fine," Spark says. "It's *supposed* to look like that."

Above the chute, the balloon tugs. Below it, Skylark hangs from its line, ready.

=== A2-CHUTE-FOLDED ===

"Leave it in the sleeve," you say. "Tidy is good."

Spark shrugs and ties the sleeved chute into the line. It hangs there between the balloon and the box like a neat red tube, perfectly still.

It looks very professional. Bitsy takes a photo of it.

"When the balloon pops," she explains to D.U.D.E.A.D., "Skylark falls, the air rushes up, and it yanks the chute out. *Fwoomp.*"

D.U.D.E.A.D. looks at the single wrap of tape around the sleeve, then at the strings coming out the bottom of it. "Fwoomp," he repeats, doubtfully.

=== A2-GONOGO ===

Spark switches Skylark on. The green light. The GPS light, blinking, searching for satellites. The camera's light, if the camera's still in there. The box is tied onto the line now. The balloon is pulling at it, and the box is pulling at Spark's hands.

"Ready," he says.

Your phone buzzes.

**Ms. Chen:** *Before you let go: what have you confirmed from a distance? — Ms. C*

You look at the box, a meter from your face. Everything you've checked so far, you've checked from right next to it. In about twenty minutes, you'll be checking from a hundred kilometers away.

Spark could carry it across the field. You could wait for the GPS to get a fix, and for Bitsy's laptop to receive a packet from a real distance. It would take a few minutes. The flag on the pole is starting to lift.

> **ENGINEERING NOTE — Ground Checks**
> Before every rocket launch, there's a go/no-go poll: every system is checked one last time, and anyone can say no. The checks are done the way the system will actually be used. For a radio, that means at a distance, not on the bench. A problem you find on the ground costs minutes. The same problem found in the air can cost the whole mission.

=== A2-CHECK ===

"Walk it out," you say.

Spark carries Skylark across the field, holding the line carefully while Mr. Torres walks the balloon along beside him. You watch them get smaller: fifty meters, seventy-five, a hundred, out by the fence.

Bitsy watches her laptop. The GPS gets its fix. Packets start to arrive from the far end of the field: position, altitude, temperature, battery.

She counts them out loud as they come in. "One. Two. Three..."

=== A2-CHECK-ANT ===

"...four. Five... Wait." Bitsy frowns. "That one's missing. And that one."

Every ten seconds, there should be a packet. At a hundred meters, about half of them aren't arriving. The ones that do arrive have a signal-strength number so low it's almost zero.

"It's the antenna," you say. "The coil."

Spark walks back with the box and carefully unwinds the coiled antenna wire. He measures 8.2 centimeters against his ruler, cuts it, pokes it through the bottom of the box and tapes it so it points straight down.

He walks it back out to the fence. Bitsy counts again. Every single packet arrives, with a signal number five times stronger than before.

"From a *hundred meters*," Bitsy says. "Imagine from a hundred *kilometers*."

Nobody wants to imagine it.

=== A2-GATE ===

Mr. Torres looks up at the flag on the fairgrounds pole. It's moving now, rippling gently, sometimes snapping out straight and then dropping again.

He licks his finger and holds it up, which Bitsy says is not scientific. He ignores her.

"Okay," he says. "Let's see where we are on time."

=== A2-GUSTY ===

The wind has arrived early, or you've arrived late. Every thirty seconds, a gust grabs the balloon and leans it over sideways, so far that Skylark swings out on its line like a pendulum and the box skims a meter above the tarp.

"Now or never," Mr. Torres says. "It's only going to get worse."

Spark is holding the box. The balloon is above him and leaning downwind. If he lets go of the box while the balloon is leaning, the balloon will drag it sideways before lifting it.

"Walk with it," D.U.D.E.A.D. says. "Get under the balloon."

"Or just let go and let it sort itself out," Bitsy says through chattering teeth.

=== A2-SNAG ===

"Let go!" you yell.

Spark lets go of the box. Mr. Torres lets go of the balloon. At that exact moment, a gust shoves the balloon sideways, and Skylark, instead of rising, is dragged along the ground behind it like a dog on a leash.

It bounces once off the frozen grass, *hard*, with a crack that makes everyone flinch. Then the balloon finds its lift and pulls it into the air.

Bitsy is staring at the patch of grass where it bounced. There's a small piece of something shiny lying there. Spark picks it up.

It's a sliver of glass. A tiny, curved piece of the camera lens.

"It's still climbing," Mr. Torres says quietly. "It's still flying."

It is. The camera on it is cracked, though.

=== A2-RELEASE ===

Skylark rises: past the top of the van, past the flagpole, up into the cold, clear sky. Once it's clear of the ground, it doesn't wobble. It just rises, steadily and silently, like an elevator with no building around it.

Everyone tips their heads back to watch.

The balloon shrinks to the size of a beach ball, then a baseball, then a white dot. Skylark is already too small to see.

Bitsy's laptop chirps. The first packet from the sky: altitude 312 meters and climbing.

"It's going," Bitsy whispers. "It's actually going."

"Then *we're* going," Mr. Torres says, already jogging to the van. "Everybody in!"
