=== E-CURVE ===

The flight log opens first. Every second of the flight is there: launch at the fairgrounds, the long climb, the temperature turning around at the tropopause, the burst at 31 kilometers, the drop, the landing. Bitsy makes a graph of altitude against time, and it's a perfect mountain: up, up, up, then a cliff.

Then the camera card. Eighteen hundred photos.

You scroll through them together. The fairgrounds getting smaller. Clouds from above. The sky turning darker blue, then navy, then...

Photo 1,412.

Nobody says anything for a long time.

The top of the picture is black. Not night-black. *Space*-black, with the sun shining. Below it is a thin, glowing line of blue: the whole atmosphere, everything that's ever breathed, in a layer thinner than a fingernail. And below that, the Earth, brown and green and white, gently and unmistakably *curved*.

Bitsy prints it on the big printer. It goes on the wall by the maker space door, where everyone will see it.

You made every call carefully, before anything could go wrong, and nothing did. That doesn't happen by luck. It happens because you tested, measured, checked, and asked what could go wrong while there was still time to do something about it.

D.U.D.E.A.D. stands in front of the photo with his arms crossed for a long time.

"Kid," he says finally. "That's the *planet*."

=== E-CURVE-CAUGHT ===

The flight log opens first: every second, launch to landing, perfect. The camera card opens next: eighteen hundred photos.

Photo 1,412 is the one. A black sky over a thin glowing line of blue, and below it, the Earth, curving away. It goes on the wall by the maker space door.

But Bitsy pins something next to it: a sheet of paper, handwritten, titled **THINGS THAT ALMOST WENT WRONG.**

On it are the problems you found before they could find you. Maybe it was a camera card that filled up during the soak test, an hour before it would have filled up in the sky. A log file that came back empty when Spark bumped the battery clip at Bitsy's kitchen table. An antenna that dropped half its packets at a hundred meters, while you could still fix it with a ruler and a pair of cutters. A power bank that tipped the scale over the limit.

Every one of those was a mistake. Every one of them was caught by a test you chose to run.

"That's the real picture," Ms. Chen says when she visits on Monday. She points to the list, not the photo. "Anybody can get lucky. You built a way to *not need* luck."

You didn't make a perfect box. You made a box, tested it, found what was wrong and fixed it. That's what engineering is.

=== E-LOWBURST ===

The flight log is perfect: every reading from launch to landing. The camera card is perfect too: eighteen hundred photos.

You scroll through them, waiting for the black sky.

It doesn't come.

The clouds fall away below. The ground gets small and hazy. The sky gets darker, a deep, rich blue, the bluest blue any of you have ever seen. And then, at the top of the climb, the photos suddenly start spinning, and it's falling.

The balloon burst at 21 kilometers, ten kilometers lower than planned.

Bitsy looks at your Mission Log. If you handled the balloon bare-handed, fingerprints left weak spots in the latex, and it couldn't stretch as far before it split. If you overfilled it, it was already big when it left the ground, so it reached its bursting size sooner. Either way, it popped early.

The pictures are still amazing. The horizon is curved, just slightly, if you look closely. The sky is darker than anyone at school has ever seen it. It goes on the wall.

But Spark keeps looking at the deep blue at the top of the frame. "Ten more kilometers," he says. "It was *right there*."

Next time: gloves, and a fill weight. The edge of space is waiting.

=== E-HALFWAY ===

The flight log is perfect. Every second, launch to landing, a perfect mountain of a graph.

Then the camera card. It holds one big video file.

You watch it together, and for the first hour it's incredible. The fairgrounds shrink. The clouds slide by underneath. The horizon starts to bend. The sky goes from light blue to deep blue to darker still, almost navy...

Then the video stops.

Sixty-one minutes. Eighteen kilometers. The card filled up, exactly like the math on the whiteboard said it would. No burst, no black sky, no curve, and no fall.

Spark puts his face in his hands. Bitsy pulls the half-finished pizza-box math out of her backpack, where it's been since last night, and looks at it for a long moment.

"8,000 divided by 130," she says. "It was right there."

The video is still beautiful. It goes on the school website, and people watch it again and again. But everyone who watches gets to the end and asks the same question: *what happened after that?*

You know what happened after that. You just can't show anyone.

Next time, you'll do the math before you choose. A data budget is like any other budget: you work it out before you spend.

=== E-NOPICS ===

The flight log opens, and it's perfect. Every second from launch to landing: altitude, temperature, pressure, position. Bitsy makes graphs. The altitude climbs like a mountain. The temperature dips and then turns around at the tropopause. It's good science. Ms. Chen says it's the best student dataset she's ever seen.

But there's no camera card.

If you took the camera out at the weigh-in, it never went up at all. If it cracked when the balloon dragged Skylark along the grass at launch, it never took a clear picture. If Skylark hit the ground at seventy kilometers an hour with its parachute stuck in a sleeve, the camera board snapped in half.

Either way, there are no pictures of the edge of space.

Bitsy prints the altitude graph and pins it on the wall by the maker space door. It's a good graph. People walk past it every day.

Nobody stops to look at it.

"Graphs are science," Spark says, a little sadly.

"Graphs are science," Bitsy agrees. "Pictures are why people *care* about science."

Next time, the camera stays in, and whatever threatened it gets fixed: the weight budget, the launch, the parachute.

=== E-NODATA ===

The camera card opens first, and it's incredible. Eighteen hundred photos, from the fairgrounds to the clouds to the black sky, and one perfect shot of the curve of the Earth.

Then Bitsy opens the flight log.

It's zero bytes.

She stares at it. She opens it again. Same thing. The file is there, with the right name and the right date, and it's completely empty.

You know why. Skylark held all its readings in a buffer, waiting to write them to the card when the END command closed the file. But END never came. The power went first: maybe you pulled the battery clip in the field, or maybe the landing knocked it loose. When the power went, everything in the buffer went with it.

So you have pictures of the edge of space, and you can't prove how high they were taken. You can't say how cold it got, or when it burst, or how fast it fell. Ms. Chen asks how high the burst was, and the honest answer is *we think about thirty kilometers*.

The photo goes on the wall anyway. It's beautiful.

But Bitsy has a new sticky note on her laptop now. It doesn't say END. It says **FLUSH**.

=== E-EMPTY ===

Bitsy puts in the first card: the flight log. Zero bytes. The file is there, but it's empty. The power went before END ever came, and two and a half hours of readings were still in the buffer when it did.

She puts in the second card: the camera. There's nothing to put in. Either the camera never flew, or it snapped in half in the landing, or it never took a clear picture after it cracked at launch.

She tries the first card again anyway.

Zero bytes.

Skylark sits on the workbench, muddy and dented. It went to the edge of space, or close to it. You chased it across two counties, found it in a field and brought it home. And it can't tell you anything about where it went. You can't prove it went anywhere at all.

Spark finally says it. "It's just a box now."

That's the hardest kind of failure: you did the hard part and lost the result. But every empty card has a reason, and you can name both of them. Flush the log after every line, so a power cut can't erase it. Protect the camera: the weight budget, the launch, the parachute.

Skylark II won't make the same mistakes. Neither will you.

=== E-SOGGY ===

The water ruined everything inside the box. The Arduino has a green crust on it. The GPS module won't turn on. The radio is dead. That's about $140 of parts that will never work again.

But memory cards are tough. Bitsy dries them carefully with a towel, lets them sit overnight in a bowl of uncooked rice, just in case, and puts them in her laptop on Monday morning.

They both open.

Every reading. Every photo. The climb, the burst, the curve of the Earth, the long fall, and the final few hundred photos, all of them of the reservoir getting closer and closer, and then a lot of murky green.

You got the science. You lost the hardware.

And you know exactly why it landed in the water: the prediction said half the landing ellipse was over the reservoir, and you launched anyway. Or you never reran the prediction with the morning's winds and never knew the reservoir was there at all. It was a coin flip, and the coin landed on the blue half.

Ms. Chen pins the burst photo on the wall. Then she pins the last photo next to it: murky green water. "Both of those are data," she says.

Next time: rerun the prediction, and when the ellipse touches water, change the flight.

=== E-SOGGY-BLANK ===

The water ruined everything. The Arduino is green with corrosion. The GPS, the radio and the sensors are all dead. That's $140 of parts.

The camera card survives. Memory cards are tough, and after a night in a bowl of rice, it opens: the climb, the clouds, the curve of the Earth, and then the reservoir rushing up, and then murky green.

The flight log card opens too. But the file on it is zero bytes.

When the water got into the box, it shorted out the batteries, and the power died. Skylark had been holding its readings in a buffer all flight, waiting for an END command to write them to the card. END never came. Nobody can send a command to a box floating in a reservoir overnight.

So you have pictures and no numbers, and a box full of ruined parts.

Two choices put you here. The landing ellipse touched the reservoir, and the flight went ahead anyway. And the log only saved itself at END, so when the power died, the data died with it.

Either one alone would have been survivable. Together, they took almost everything.

Bitsy writes on the whiteboard: *RERUN THE PREDICTION. FLUSH THE LOG.* Then she underlines both.

=== E-TREE ===

The sun goes down with Skylark still hanging in the pine tree, twelve meters up, its parachute wrapped tight around the branch.

Mr. Okafor, who lives across the road, comes out to watch you pack up. "I'll keep an eye on it," he promises. "If it ever comes down, I'll call."

It doesn't come down. Not that week, or the next. Snow comes, and the box hangs in the tree all winter, a little dark shape swaying in the wind.

In March, a spring storm finally knocks it loose. Mr. Okafor calls the school. When you get it back, it's been rained on, snowed on and frozen solid more times than anyone can count. The foam is cracked. The electronics are ruined. The memory cards open, but there are a few photos of the tree, and then nothing, because the batteries died in November.

The data from the flight itself is there: the climb, the burst, the fall. But it's five months late, and the science fair was in January.

Spark is quiet for a long time. Then he says, "We traded a lake for a forest."

He's right. Adding helium moved the landing away from the water and toward the trees. Then the clock ran out before you could get it down. There were ways to do both, and you know what they are now.

=== E-COWS ===

You come back on Sunday with Ms. Chen. You knock on the farmhouse door first this time. Mrs. Lindqvist listens to your apology, and Ms. Chen's, and finally nods. "All right," she says. "Go find your box."

But overnight, she moved her cattle into that field. It was planned weeks ago. It's what the field is for after the harvest.

Skylark is in the middle of it. It's been stepped on, rolled over, chewed and, from the look of it, licked extensively. The foam box is in pieces. The radio board is in a cow pie. The camera is missing completely. The SD card from the logger is snapped in half.

Mrs. Lindqvist looks at the remains with her arms crossed. "If you'd knocked yesterday," she says, not unkindly, "I'd have driven you out there myself. My grandson would have loved it."

That's the part that stings. The farmer was never the problem. She would have helped. She only needed to be asked.

Five minutes saved by hopping a fence cost a whole night, and the whole night cost everything. Out in the real world, a field always belongs to somebody, and asking first is part of the mission.

D.U.D.E.A.D. carries the pieces back to the van. "Lesson learned, kid," he says. "Knock."

=== E-FLOATER ===

The last packet from Skylark comes from somewhere over the next state, at 24 kilometers, heading east at ninety kilometers an hour. After that, nothing. It drifted out of range.

It never burst. There wasn't enough helium in the balloon to lift it high enough to stretch to its bursting size, so it found a height where everything balanced, and it just stayed there, riding the jet stream.

It might still be up there tonight. It might drift for days, across states, maybe across the ocean, as the helium slowly leaks out and it sinks lower and lower. It might come down in a forest in Canada, or in the Atlantic, or in a field in a country you've never heard of. Nobody will ever know.

You watch the empty laptop screen for a long time.

"How much helium would it have taken?" Spark asks finally.

Bitsy doesn't need to look it up. "Enough to lift the fill weight," she says. "The jug. It was right there on the table."

Measuring would have taken two minutes. Guessing took the whole mission.

If you wrote a label on it, maybe, someday, someone will call. But a box that lands three countries away probably isn't going to come home.

Next time, you'll measure the free lift. You'll never eyeball anything important again.

=== E-RETURN ===

For three weeks, nothing.

Bitsy checks her phone every hour. Spark builds a new box, then takes it apart again. D.U.D.E.A.D. stops asking.

Then, on a Thursday afternoon, the school office calls Ms. Chen's classroom. There's a farmer on the phone. He found a foam box in his field while he was spreading fertilizer, with **HARMLESS SCIENCE EXPERIMENT** written on the side in marker, and this phone number.

"It says *reward*," he says. "What's the reward?"

You deliver the pizza personally.

Skylark is in rough shape. Three weeks of rain and frost have cracked the foam. The batteries are completely dead. The electronics might be okay, or might not. But the memory cards are fine. The data and photos from the flight are all there, every second of whatever Skylark saw before it went quiet.

You lost it because of choices you made: the batteries, the clock, the search. You got it back because of one choice you almost didn't make. The marker was right there in the cup. It took two minutes.

"A label," Bitsy says, shaking her head. "Of everything we did, the *label* saved us."

D.U.D.E.A.D. shrugs. "You can't always find your stuff, kid. But you can make it easy for someone else to."

=== E-LOST ===

Skylark is out there somewhere.

It's in a field, or a ditch, or a patch of woods, sitting quietly with its dead batteries and its memory cards full of pictures nobody will ever see. Maybe a farmer will find it next spring and wonder what it is. Maybe they'll throw it away. There's nothing on it to say what it is, or whose it is, or who to call.

Nobody calls.

You go back over every choice that led here. Maybe Skylark went silent: cold batteries with nothing to keep them warm, or a reboot at minus fifty. Maybe the clock ran out: a search that took too long, or a field you couldn't see well enough. Every one of those choices made it a little harder to find, until it couldn't be found at all.

And there's one choice that would have saved it even after all the others: a label. A phone number, in marker, on the side of the box. Someone, someday, would have called.

Spark starts a list for Skylark II. At the very top, in capital letters, he writes: **LABEL.**

It's the worst ending. It's also one you'll never get twice, because now you know exactly what each of those choices costs. That's not nothing. That's how every engineer who ever lost something learned not to.

=== E-GROUNDED ===

At 10:30, the wind arrives, right on schedule.

The first gust hits the balloon like a hand slapping a beach ball. It leans so far over that Skylark drags across the tarp, and Mr. Torres has to grab the line with both hands. The second gust is worse. The balloon thrashes, folding and unfolding, and for a second, everyone thinks it's going to rip.

"That's it," Mr. Torres says. "We're not launching in this. It's not safe for the balloon, and it's not safe for anyone holding it."

Nobody argues. You can all see it.

Here's the worst part: you can't put the helium back. A filled balloon is filled. You hold it down in the wind for a while, hoping, but the gusts don't stop. Finally, Mr. Torres lets the helium out through the valve, slowly, and the balloon wrinkles and sags and settles back onto the tarp like a deflated bedsheet.

Skylark never leaves the ground.

You can count exactly where the time went: every Launch box you crossed out. Some of those were good choices, the kind that make a flight safer. But all together, they cost more time than the morning had.

"Next time," Bitsy says quietly, "we get here at six."

The helium tank is empty, and the balloon is used up. It'll be months before you can try again.
